There’s something worth noticing in your last sentence: you already identified the core problem. You’re grading yourself against a rubric you didn’t write — reproduction, conventional success markers — and then suffering when you fail it. The geese aren’t “succeeding” at anything; they’re just being geese. You’re the only one in that scene running a scoring algorithm, and the algorithm is the problem, not your life.

The daily drive is doing something specific: it’s a cue-routine loop. Same road, same trigger, same spiral, every morning. Rumination is partly a habit with a location and a schedule, and that’s actually good news because habits can be disrupted mechanically before they can be resolved philosophically. Some people break the loop by changing the input during the trigger window — a podcast that demands attention, calling someone, even a different route. Others use a labeling practice: when the spiral starts, you name it (“ah, the morning self-audit again”) rather than arguing with it. Naming a thought as a recurring rerun, rather than fresh evidence, robs it of authority. You don’t have to win the argument; you just have to stop treating the prosecutor as credible.

Two things I’d say honestly, though. First, the resentment toward your partner is a live wire running through this, and a thought-pattern fix won’t touch it — that one deserves direct attention, possibly conversation with her, possibly with a counselor. Daily resentment corrodes whether or not it’s justified. Second, the depth and dailiness of what you’re describing — years of self-attack on a loop — is exactly the kind of thing a good therapist is genuinely effective at, not as a crisis measure but as a craftsman for this specific job. You’re clearly self-aware and articulate; that makes the work go faster, not slower.

The reframe you’re reaching for — appreciating what you are, redefining the remaining years on your own terms — isn’t achieved by thinking harder. It’s achieved by repeatedly catching the old rubric in the act and declining to use it, plus building actual evidence of a life you value: small commitments kept, things made, people engaged. What would a quarter of a life designed by you actually contain? That’s a more interesting morning question than the one the geese keep asking.
